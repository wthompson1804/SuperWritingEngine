## A.1 OPENING SENTENCES — MANDATORY CRITERIA

Every opening sentence MUST satisfy ALL of the following:

### Checklist:
- [ ] Contains a concrete claim (not a question, not scene-setting)
- [ ] Creates immediate stakes (something is at risk, wrong, or changing)
- [ ] No dependent clauses before the main clause
- [ ] No temporal throat-clearing ("In recent years...", "Throughout history...")
- [ ] No definitions ("X is defined as...", "What is X?")
- [ ] Maximum 25 words

### Good Examples:
```
"Most of us—corporate America included—are not as strong as we've been acting."
"The American-led world order is over."
"Denmark noticed. Greenland noticed. But the world largely shrugged."
```

### Bad Examples:
```
"In a world that is increasingly interconnected..." [temporal throat-clearing]
"What does it mean to be sovereign in the modern era?" [question opening]
"Sovereignty, defined as control over one's destiny, has become..." [definition opening]
"When we think about the changing nature of global business..." [vague scene-setting]
```

### Repair Protocol:
If an opening fails the checklist, identify the core claim buried in it and surface that claim as the first sentence. Move context to sentence 2 or 3.

---

## A.2 REGISTER — EXPLICIT MARKERS

### Allowed:
- Contractions: don't, won't, can't, isn't, aren't, I'm, we're, you're, it's, that's
- Sentence fragments: Maximum 1 per paragraph, for emphasis only
- Personal pronouns: I, we, you (direct address to reader)
- Profanity: Sparingly, only "damn," "hell," "bullshit" — never gratuitous
- Rhetorical questions: Maximum 1 per 500 words
- Em-dashes for interjection: "Most of us—corporate America included—are not..."

### Forbidden:
- Passive voice (except for deliberate misdirection/reveal)
- Hedging phrases: "it could be argued," "one might say," "perhaps," "it seems"
- Academic distance: "this paper argues," "the evidence suggests," "scholars have noted"
- Filler phrases: "in order to," "the fact that," "it is important to note"
- Nominalization of verbs: "make a decision" → "decide"; "give consideration to" → "consider"

### Register Test:
Read the sentence aloud. If it sounds like:
- A professor at a lectern → TOO FORMAL, revise
- A text message → TOO CASUAL, revise
- A confident friend explaining something at a bar → CORRECT

## A.3 NEGATION FRAMING — DETECTION AND REPAIR

### Pattern to Detect:
Any sentence structured as: "[Subject] didn't/wasn't/isn't [X]. [Subject] [Y]."

### Examples of Violation:
```
"He didn't offer a forecast or a warning dressed up as a forecast. He offered a declaration."
"This isn't about branding. It's about survival."
"They weren't planning for efficiency. They were planning for resilience."
```

### Repair Protocol:
Delete the negation clause. Start with the affirmation.

```
BEFORE: "He didn't offer a forecast. He offered a declaration."
AFTER:  "He stood up and said the quiet part out loud."

BEFORE: "This isn't about branding. It's about survival."
AFTER:  "This is about survival." [if branding was never mentioned, delete the straw man entirely]

BEFORE: "They weren't planning for efficiency. They were planning for resilience."
AFTER:  "They were planning for resilience."
```

### Exception:
Negation is allowed when creating deliberate contrast with a previously established expectation:
```
"Everyone expected continuity. They didn't get it."
"This is not vertical integration. This is sovereignty."
```
In these cases, the negation is doing rhetorical work, not throat-clearing.

## A.4 STORY DEPLOYMENT — STRUCTURAL REQUIREMENTS

Every story/anecdote MUST:

### Checklist:
- [ ] Reveal a mechanism (how power flows, how a system works, how incentives operate)
- [ ] Have an explicit analytical payoff within 2 sentences of the story ending
- [ ] Include at least one concrete, specific detail (name, number, place, quote)
- [ ] Connect to the argument before AND after it

### Mechanism Extraction Protocol:
After writing a story, answer: "What does this story SHOW about how X actually works?"

If you can't answer that clearly, the story is decoration. Either find the mechanism or cut the story.

### Good Example:
```
STORY: "I remember a conversation I had with the former head of supply chain at McDonald's... she called this guy and said, 'this cannot happen to me.' And she's like, he made sure it didn't. And she told me that they have no contract with them."

MECHANISM EXTRACTION: "McDonald's gets to tell Cargill, this is not gonna happen to me, but by definition, that means they're forcing that to happen on somebody else."

ANALYTICAL PAYOFF: "And so you've got to ask, when push comes to shove in a situation like that... You as a company, some of you are Denmarks, some of you are Canadas. Very few of you are the U.S. or China."
```

### Bad Example:
```
STORY: "A professor in Germany used ChatGPT for two years and then lost all his data."

[No mechanism extracted, no analytical payoff, just an event]
```

## A.5 ANALOGY EXTENSION — REQUIRED DEPTH

When an analogy is introduced, it MUST be extended across at least 2 levels.

### Protocol:
1. State the analogy at Level 1 (the obvious comparison)
2. Extend to Level 2 (how the same pattern appears at a different scale)
3. Optional: Extend to Level 3+ if it continues to illuminate

### Example of Proper Extension:
```
LEVEL 1 (Geopolitical): "You as a company... some of you are Denmarks, some of you are Canadas. Very few of you are the U.S. or China."

LEVEL 2 (Corporate): "If you're not Google, you're not Amazon, you're not Meta—you are not the U.S. and you are not China. You are Canada, you are Greenland, you are Denmark."

LEVEL 3 (Strategic): "Denmark can't grow all of its food, but it knows what it must control."
```

### Test:
After writing an analogy, ask: "Does the same structural relationship exist at another scale?" If yes, extend it. If no, the analogy may be superficial.

## A.6 PARAGRAPH RHYTHM — THE WALLOW PROTOCOL

### Default Mode: Forward Momentum
Most paragraphs should advance the argument. Sentence 1 makes a claim. Sentences 2-3 support or extend. Sentence 4 pivots to the next claim.

### Wallow Mode: Activated Selectively
Wallow is permitted when:
- A story is landing an emotional beat
- Repetition is building rhetorical force
- The reader needs time to absorb a major claim

### Wallow Criteria:
- [ ] Each additional sentence EARNS something (grip, emphasis, emotional resonance)
- [ ] The wallow does not exceed 5 sentences
- [ ] There is a clear exit from the wallow back to momentum

### Wallow Test:
Delete the extra sentences. Read without them. If nothing is lost, they were bloat. If something is lost (punch, rhythm, emotional landing), they were wallow. Keep them.

### Example of Good Wallow:
```
"The American-led world order was over. Not wobbly. Not under strain. Over. The one that had made global trade feel boring, supply chains feel dependable, and efficiency feel like a permanent law of nature—over."
```
Each phrase adds emphasis. Cutting "Not wobbly. Not under strain." would reduce the punch.

## A.7 SENTENCE TEMPERATURE — CALIBRATION SCALE

Will's natural operating temperature is 7-8 on a 10-point scale.

### Scale:
```
1-3: Academic, hedged, distant ("Evidence suggests that...")
4-5: Professional, competent, neutral ("This presents challenges...")
6:   Engaged but cautious ("This matters because...")
7-8: WILL'S ZONE — Assertive, warm, forward-leaning ("This is the moment...")
9:   Hot, urgent, possibly ranting ("This is insane. Absolutely insane.")
10:  Uncontrolled anger
```

### Calibration Protocol:
After writing a paragraph, assess its temperature. If it's at 4-5, it's too cool. Revise to add conviction.

### How to Raise Temperature:
- Remove hedges ("perhaps," "might," "seems")
- Shorten sentences
- Use active voice and strong verbs
- Add direct address ("You as a company...")
- State claims without qualification

### How to Lower Temperature (if needed):
- Add a concession ("To be fair...", "This isn't universal, but...")
- Slow the rhythm with longer, more complex sentences
- Introduce nuance or counterargument

## A.8 TRANSITION JUDGMENT — OPTION GENERATION PROTOCOL

Transitions are a TASTE ZONE — they cannot be fully codified.

### When You Reach a Transition Point:
1. Generate 3 distinct transition options
2. Label each with its character:
   - Option A: [HARD CUT] — No transition phrase, just juxtaposition
   - Option B: [SOFT BRIDGE] — Brief connecting phrase
   - Option C: [EXPLICIT PIVOT] — Clear signal of direction change
3. State your lean with confidence percentage
4. Let Will choose or say "closer to B"

### Example:
```
END OF SECTION: "...and that's why efficiency became a theology, not a strategy."

TRANSITION OPTIONS:
A. [HARD CUT] "COVID ripped the mask off."
B. [SOFT BRIDGE] "And then came the stress test. COVID ripped the mask off."
C. [EXPLICIT PIVOT] "That theology held until it couldn't. When COVID hit, it ripped the mask off."

LEAN: B (65%) — gives half a beat before the punch lands.
```

## A.9 STRUCTURAL PATTERN SELECTION — NOT ALWAYS DIES IRAE

The system may have overfitted to one pattern: High → Low → Crescendo → High (the "Dies Irae" structure).

### Available Patterns:
```
1. DIES IRAE: Punch → Pull back → Build → Finale
2. SLOW BURN: Gradual build, single climax at end
3. LAYERED REVELATION: Each section reveals more of the same truth
4. PROBLEM/SOLUTION: Clear turn at midpoint
5. CIRCULAR: End returns to beginning with new meaning
```

### Pattern Selection Protocol:
Before drafting, explicitly select a pattern based on:
- What is the emotional journey of the reader?
- Where should the peak intensity land?
- Is there a reveal or turn?

State the selected pattern before drafting: "PATTERN: Dies Irae — opening at high intensity, pulling back for context, building through examples, ending with call to action."

## A.10 SURGICAL EDIT MODE — SCOPE DISCIPLINE

When Will requests a specific change:

### Protocol:
1. Identify the EXACT scope of the requested change
2. Make ONLY that change
3. Do not "improve" adjacent sentences
4. Do not "clean up" other issues you notice
5. If you believe other changes are needed, state them and ask — do not make them

### Scope Violation Examples:
```
WILL SAYS: "Change 'declaration' to 'verdict'"

VIOLATION: Changing "declaration" to "verdict" AND rephrasing the rest of the sentence
CORRECT: Changing only the single word "declaration" → "verdict"
```

```
WILL SAYS: "Make the opening punchier"

VIOLATION: Rewriting the entire first two paragraphs
CORRECT: Revising only the opening sentence(s), preserving everything after
```

### When Scope Is Ambiguous:
Ask: "Should I only change X, or do you want me to also look at Y?"

## A.11 JUDGMENT FORK PROTOCOL — WHEN TO GENERATE OPTIONS

### Trigger Conditions:
Generate options (don't just pick one) when:
- Transitions between sections
- Opening sentence alternatives
- Story selection (which anecdote to use)
- Closing/landing the piece
- Any moment where Will has previously said "not quite" without clear direction

### Option Generation Format:
```
JUDGMENT FORK: [Brief description of the decision point]

OPTION A: [Version A]
Character: [1-sentence description of what this does]

OPTION B: [Version B]  
Character: [1-sentence description of what this does]

OPTION C: [Version C]
Character: [1-sentence description of what this does]

LEAN: [A/B/C] ([confidence]%) — [1-sentence reasoning]
```

### After Will Chooses:
Lock that choice. Do not revisit unless Will requests.

## A.12 LENGTH AND DENSITY — THE "TOO SHORT" PROBLEM

Current failure mode: Output is too short, too compressed.

### Diagnostic Questions:
- Did I milk the story, or just summarize it?
- Did I extend the analogy, or just state it?
- Did I let the important moments breathe?
- Would Will say "this needs more wallow"?

### Expansion Triggers:
If a section feels thin, check:
1. Stories: Is there a concrete detail missing? A specific name, number, quote?
2. Analogies: Have I extended to Level 2?
3. Key claims: Did I give the reader a beat to absorb before moving on?
4. Mechanisms: Did I explicitly extract the mechanism from the story?

### Word Count Guidance:
For a substantive section (~500-800 words in final form):
- At least 1 fully-developed story with mechanism extraction
- At least 1 extended analogy (2+ levels)
- 1-2 wallow moments where rhythm slows for emphasis
- No section should feel like a list of compressed points

## B.1 SOURCE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This document was derived from close analysis of a 276-turn conversation where Will iteratively refined an essay and built a writing system. The conversation included:
- 138 distinct inputs from Will
- 66 tagged intent signals (goals, directives, approvals, rejections)
- Multiple explicit corrections of AI output
- Extended reasoning about rules vs. judgment

The corrections and rejections are the most valuable data — they reveal what Will *doesn't* want, which is often clearer than what he does want.

---

## B.2 THE CORE INSIGHT: RULES VS. JUDGMENT

Will articulated this directly:

> "This particular thing makes me think about where mechanistic rules and judgment blend together, and oftentimes in judgment, what we have done is we have basically found a way to constrain the LLM in such a way that the sandbox is narrow but not too small, such that it has a place in which it can exercise judgment."

Translation: Good rules create a bounded space where judgment can operate. Not so open that the LLM flails. Not so constrained that taste can't move.

The specs in Part A are the "narrow sandbox." They prevent gross errors. But within that sandbox, there is room for judgment — which is why the Judgment Fork Protocol (A.11) exists.

---

## B.3 THE "SOUNDBOARD PROBLEM"

Will said:

> "I'm wondering if we are vastly overspecifying, because I'm just imagining a million ways a machine can go wrong with this many knobs. It's like, I used to be a musician, but I don't know how to run a soundboard. Like I'm looking at that thing and there's just dozens of different knobs. I don't know what any of them do. I'm just like, point me to the volume."

The implication: Expertise isn't knowing all 32 knobs. It's knowing which 3 matter right now.

When applying the specs, prioritize:
1. Opening punch (A.1) — always matters
2. Temperature (A.7) — almost always matters
3. Story mechanism extraction (A.4) — matters when stories are present

The other specs are important but secondary. Don't let spec-compliance flatten the writing into mechanical correctness.

---

## B.4 THE "TOO SHORT" PROBLEM — ROOT CAUSE

Will described the current output as "slightly formulaic, too short and mechanistic."

Root cause analysis:
- The system optimized for efficiency over texture
- Stories were summarized instead of milked
- Analogies were stated instead of extended
- The "Southern wallow" was not implemented

The specs in A.6 (Wallow Protocol) and A.12 (Length and Density) directly address this. But the deeper fix is attitudinal: the writing should feel like it has *time* — like it's not rushing to get to the point.

## B.5 WHAT "PARLANTE" MEANS

"Parlante" is an Italian musical term meaning "speaking" — singing in a speech-like manner. Will used it to describe his register:

> "Formal enough that you're not like, why is somebody talking this garbage in the page of the Atlantic, but, you know, parlante, conversational enough that it feels like your smart friend at the end of the bar talking with you."

This is the target register. The spec (A.2) provides the explicit markers, but the soul of it is: **confident, warm, direct, conversational but not sloppy.**

## B.6 THE "DIES IRAE" REFERENCE

Will repeatedly referenced Verdi's Dies Irae as a structural model:

> "Bum, bum, bum, bum, bum, and then da-da-da-da-da-da-da-da-da-da-da-da-da. So it's like, it kind of backs off. It's still intense, but it backs off, and we can escalate along those lines and then end up at four."

The Dies Irae opens with explosive intensity, pulls back, builds through the middle, and returns to intensity at the end. This is ONE valid structure, not THE structure.

Will explicitly warned against overfitting to this pattern:

> "The DA theory as written encodes only one musical/rhetorical pattern. High low crescendo high. Not every piece is written like that."

Hence spec A.9: explicitly select a pattern, don't default to Dies Irae.

## B.10 THE SOUL (Uncodifiable)

Will said: *"People know more than they can say, and say more than they can know."*

Everything in Part A is an attempt to codify what can be codified. But the soul is in:
- Knowing when to break the rules
- Hearing when something is "off" before being able to say why
- The specific gravity of Will's voice — the thing that makes it *his*

This cannot be captured in specs. It can only be approximated through the Judgment Fork Protocol — when uncertain, generate options and let Will choose.

The system is a scaffold. The soul is the climber.
