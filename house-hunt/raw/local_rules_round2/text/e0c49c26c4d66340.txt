2024 Winter - Goutweed
Aegopodium podagraria
Common names include: Goutweed and Snow-on-the-mountain
This perennial member of the Parsley family was introduced from Europe and Northern Asia in the mid 1800's.  In the Middle Ages, monks cultivated it for treating gout, hence the name.  It thrives in moist, well drained soils in partial sun to full shade.  It reaches 4-12 inches in height, and leaf edges are often white.  Flowers typically appear in June on 18-inch-tall stalks.  Flowers are small and white in dense, flat-topped clusters 3" wide, similar to that of its relative Queen Anne's Lace.
Once goutweed is established, it becomes highly aggressive. Its underground rhizomes creep throughout the garden, and within a short time the foot-tall foliage will engulf anything planted nearby. The underground stems of goutweed work themselves among the roots of other plants and are hard to dig out without breaking off bits that can easily sprout new plantlets. When it escapes cultivation, goutweed readily grows under forest trees and eventually pushes out native plants reducing the natural diversity of the ecosystem.
The green-and-white cultivar ‘Variegatum’ was introduced as a less aggressive ornamental groundcover. However, this cultivar’s white coloring tends to disappear over time, and the plant reverts to its all-green, more invasive habit. Despite being listed as an invasive in a number of states, including Connecticut, Michigan, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Vermont, and Wisconsin, goutweed is still widely available in nurseries.
This aggressive grower can
displace native species and inhibit the establishment of conifers and other native trees. Control methods include spraying with herbicides, manually digging or hand-pulling of the plants, and continuously mowing the growth.  The most effective means of eliminating goutweed would be to cut to the ground after it has just fully leafed out, then cover with black plastic. This will prevent photosynthesizing, and deplete the roots of nutrients.
