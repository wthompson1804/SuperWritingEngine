# UX brief from Will (2026-11-15), saved verbatim

**The reframe: she isn't choosing an apartment. She's choosing what her first year on her own feels like, and how far from home to live it.**

**Who she is** (a working read built from your account; she should get to disagree)
- **Identity:** her priorities shift daily because she's trying on different selves: the social one, the safe-and-cozy one, the near-family one. That's exploring, not dithering.
- **Two pulls:** independence and closeness to her parents. Distance is the emotional axis, not a logistics number.
- **Control:** safety, the gate and the car are one need, which is feeling in control when she's alone. The car is her freedom and her safe way home.
- **Overload:** she can handle every fact. What stalls her is holding them all at once for a choice that feels permanent. It isn't permanent; a lease is a 12-month trial, and the page should say so.

**The interface: three floors and a table**
1. **Lives.** Five or six portals, each titled as a year ("The Beach Town Year"). Each tells the same four moments in second person:
   - a workday at home
   - the 9pm dog walk
   - Saturday night out, and parking when she gets back
   - Sunday at her parents', door to door with tolls

   Each portal then shows what that year costs: money, distance, and its honest catch. The research already gathers what all four moments need.
2. **Neighborhoods** in that life, from any state. Each has "You'll love / Gives us pause", safety and car-crime facts, residents' voices, a small map with her parents pinned, and "what $2,400 buys here".
3. **Units.** The existing tour cards and for-rent sign, each with a "Hold this" button.
4. **Side by side.** Her two or three held places.
   - Rows are the four moments plus money and space, not a spec sheet.
   - Rows where the places tie disappear.
   - Each pair gets one sentence in real units: "Same rent. B gives you 250 more square feet and a garage. It costs you 30 more minutes each way to your parents."

**Four sliders, as limits not weights:** monthly cost, minutes to Mom and Dad, safety, space (the last two are your call). Dragging dims what falls outside, and each life shows its count drop from "7 places" to "2". She sees what every limit costs her, with no ranking engine.

**Snapshot:** no learning and no refresh. Prices carry a date. The finish line is two or three places to go see, with the tour questions.

**Why it's built this way**
- A side-by-side table carries a known hazard: small countable gaps look bigger there than they will feel once she is living in only one of the places. Differences of amount, not of kind, are where that inflation showed up, and a later replication backed it only in part. So the table compares moments, not specs.
- The clearest evidence on what people stop noticing comes from commuting research: a raise lost its glow in German panel data, and the commute did not. My extension, possible but not proven for apartments: finishes fade, and recurring friction like parking or an uneasy night walk doesn't.
- Decision-aid researchers treat stories as a live risk, because the same facts steer choices differently once they are wrapped in a narrative. Every life gets the same four scenes, the same length and its real catch, or the best-written one wins.

**What you need to hear**
- **The profile is secondhand.** Only she can confirm two things: whether those are her four moments, and whether she wants more distance from home or less. Until then the distance slider gets no red or green.
- **Recommendations:** you earlier wanted dynamic ones. I'm reading "exploratory, not recommender" as dropping them, apart from labels like "most space for the money". Say if that over-reads you.
- **Philadelphia** isn't in the five research runs. If it's still live, it needs adding.
- **Dropped:** the one-at-a-time tour, the wish buttons and learned preferences.
